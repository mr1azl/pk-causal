Germany and Canada together have produced $279,000 of incremental revenue

Germany is at +70% revenue over 68 days and Canada at +153% over 34, both far beyond chance. They ran on different value models, in different regions, with separate controls, which makes this a replication rather than one market’s good quarter. Every market without exception shows the same mechanism underneath: fewer flight searches bought, at a higher price, converting better. No market has yet produced a revenue result that argues against the change.

The 20 August budget cut

Thirteen days before the September switch, an account-wide decision removed most of the non-brand budget in both GCC markets on the same day. It sits inside the pre-period of any model fitted across it, so the headline Saudi and Pakistan figures above measure the cut as much as the bidding. The excluded-campaign view is the one to read.

Trajectory

Germany is still improving ten weeks in, which argues against reading the early gain as a novelty effect. Pakistan’s traffic collapse has reversed on its own as the bidding settled.

Germany is accelerating, not fading

Revenue averaged $31,595 a week across the first four weeks after the switch and $45,602 across the most recent four, a 44% improvement. The strongest single week was $71,334 on a 2.07% booking rate. Volume has not grown; the weekly IBE count is flat at around 2,040. The gain is entirely in what each search is worth.

Pakistan’s traffic has come back

Weekly IBE visits fell to 143 at the trough in mid-September and are back to 535, nearly four times the low. Cost per click peaked at $1.66 in the first week after the switch and is now $0.28, below where it started. The bidding has settled. What has not yet recovered is revenue, which is why Pakistan stays unresolved.

Saudi Arabia has flipped

At the previous read Saudi’s untouched campaigns showed noise with intervals spanning 661%. They now show +89% revenue and +121% bookings, both significant. The weekly booking rate backs it up: 0.83% before the switch, then 1.45% to 2.38% every week since. The point estimates are still inflated, but the direction is no longer ambiguous.

Canada’s first week was its best

Canada peaked at $54,583 in week one and has run $29,000 to $49,000 since. That is a learning period settling rather than a decline, but it is why Canada’s +153% sits above Germany’s +70% and why the Canada figure should be expected to come down as the window lengthens.

Read before quoting any of this

Withdrawn: Pakistan’s revenue loss

An earlier read put Pakistan at −52% revenue, the only significant loss in the programme. That model’s pre-period spanned the 20 August budget cut. Corrected, Pakistan is −22% with an interval from −61% to +290% and a cross-check of +14%. Not decidable. The loss was a budget decision, not the bidding.

Withdrawn: the route-level findings

A per-market analysis joined campaign names to the route value table. The tag values the route the user searched, not the route named in the campaign: a single ISB–LGW campaign carried six different per-search values from $0.50 to $84.08. That join was invalid and everything built on it has been dropped. Nothing on this page depends on it.

Withdrawn: the Pakistan value correction

Pakistan’s flight-search value looked 7 to 11 times overstated, and a 7x reduction was nearly actioned. It was benchmarked against Floodlight revenue while the model is built from Adobe. Against Adobe, Pakistan sits at 0.91x and Germany at 1.29x, so Germany is the overstated market. Adobe revenue per ticket is $272 in Germany against $278 in Pakistan.

Two revenue systems disagree

Floodlight captures roughly 40% of Germany’s Adobe revenue and roughly 16% of Pakistan’s. Everything on this page is measured on Floodlight, consistently on both sides of every comparison, so the relative results hold. But Pakistan’s absolute revenue series is a narrow slice of the market, which is part of why it is so hard to resolve.

Singapore cannot be measured

Singapore produces 0.69 bookings a day, 47 in the entire 68-day post-period. One of its three models failed to converge, returning an interval in the hundreds of trillions. No amount of additional weeks fixes this. If Singapore needs an answer it has to come from pooling with other small markets, not from measuring it alone.

Germany lost a day on 30 September

Non-brand recorded 276 clicks against a typical 900, cost of $212 against $450, and no bookings, while brand and Performance Max ran normally. It looks like a serving or budget interruption. One day in 68, no effect on the result, but it is the visible dip at the right-hand end of the Germany chart.

Switch dates are taken as given

Campaign bid strategy in this report is not historical: every VBB portfolio shows spend back to early May, long before any market switched. It identifies which campaigns are treated; it cannot date the change. The 30 July and 2 September dates come from the team, not from the data.

The control is imperfect

Brand predicts non-brand’s daily movement poorly, with pre-period correlations between 0.02 and 0.46. That is why the intervals stay wide and the minimum detectable effects stay large. The difference-in-differences cross-check uses no model at all, so where the two agree the result does not depend on modelling choices.

Method: Bayesian structural time series (local level plus regression on the control and day of week), fitted on the pre-period by maximum likelihood, counterfactual from the filter run forward with the treated series hidden, parameter uncertainty from a stationary block bootstrap. Difference-in-differences on ratio of totals shown alongside as an independent cross-check. Pre-period starts 13 June in every market. Post-period runs from each market’s switch date to 5 October. Germany and Singapore switched 30 July; Canada, Malaysia, Saudi Arabia and Pakistan on 2 September.
